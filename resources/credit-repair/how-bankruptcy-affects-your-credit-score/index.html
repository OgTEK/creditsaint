Bankruptcy usually causes a significant drop in your credit score and stays on your credit report for years: up to 10 years for Chapter 7 and generally 7 years for Chapter 13.

Its effect fades over time, especially as you build new on-time payment history. How much it affects you depends on the rest of your credit profile.

Bankruptcy is a legal process that helps people deal with debts they can no longer pay. It offers a kind of financial reset, but it also leaves a serious mark on your report that can affect access to loans, credit cards and housing for years.

Key Takeaways
  • Payment history accounts for about 35% of a FICO Score, which makes your credit habits after bankruptcy the most important factor you control.
  • Chapter 7 bankruptcy can stay on a credit report for up to 10 years. Chapter 13 generally comes off after 7 years from the filing date under bureau practice, though the law allows up to 10.
  • A bankruptcy entry isn’t always reported accurately. Inaccurate or unverifiable entries may be eligible for dispute under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA).
  • The effect of a bankruptcy fades as it ages, especially as you add new on-time payments and keep balances low.

How Long Does Bankruptcy Stay on Your Credit Report?

The two most common types of consumer bankruptcy stay on your report for different lengths of time:

  • Chapter 7 bankruptcy sells most non-exempt assets to pay creditors. It can stay on a credit report for up to 10 years from the filing date.
  • Chapter 13 bankruptcy sets up a repayment plan over three to five years. The major bureaus generally remove it seven years from the filing date, although the FCRA allows up to 10.

While either entry is on your report, it tells lenders you went through a period of serious financial distress. That makes new credit harder to get and often more expensive when it’s offered. To learn how public records like bankruptcies are reported, see our guide on public records on your credit report.

What Affects How Your Credit Recovers After Bankruptcy?

A bankruptcy doesn’t freeze your score in place. Rebuilding can start after discharge, though the pace depends on several factors:

  • Your habits after filing: How you manage new credit after bankruptcy has a direct effect. On-time payments and low credit utilization on new accounts are the most useful tools you have.
  • Type of bankruptcy: Chapter 13 usually comes off your report sooner than Chapter 7.
  • Age of the entry: The older the bankruptcy, the less weight it carries in your score. A five-year-old bankruptcy with several years of positive history reads very differently from a recent filing.
  • Accuracy of the entry: Not every bankruptcy-related entry is reported correctly. Discharged accounts that still show balances, or entries with wrong dates, may be eligible for dispute.

How Do You Check That a Bankruptcy Is Reported Accurately?

Check that the filing and every account it included are reported correctly. Common errors include discharged debts that still show a balance, wrong dates and duplicate entries. You can start by getting all three of your credit reports.

If you find an error, you can dispute it with each bureau for free under the Fair Credit Reporting Act. The bureau generally has 30 days to investigate, up to 45 in some cases. An accurate bankruptcy can stay for its full reporting period.

Does the Impact of Bankruptcy Fade Over Time?

Yes. A bankruptcy has an immediate and significant impact, but its effect on your score usually shrinks over time. As new positive history builds alongside it, such as on-time payments, low utilization and responsible credit use, those newer signals shape more of your score.

Someone who filed Chapter 7 several years ago and has managed new accounts well since discharge may have a much higher score than someone who filed recently with no new positive history.

Over time, lenders tend to focus more on recent behavior than on the bankruptcy itself. For more on your options, see our guide on how to address bankruptcies on your credit report.

How Can You Rebuild Credit After Bankruptcy?

There’s no single path. The right approach depends on your full credit picture. That said, these tools and habits are commonly used to build new positive history after a discharge:

  • Secured credit cards: These require a cash deposit that usually serves as your credit limit. Because the lender’s risk is lower, they’re easier to get after bankruptcy. Keeping balances low and paying on time adds positive payment history. Learn how a secured card works.
  • Credit-builder loans: Offered by some credit unions and community banks, these are designed to help people build or rebuild payment history. The loan amount is held in a savings account and released after you finish paying. Payments are reported to the credit bureaus.
  • On-time payments on every account: Payment history is the single largest factor in FICO scoring. Every on-time payment on a post-bankruptcy account adds to it.
  • Low credit utilization: Keeping balances well below your limits is consistently linked to stronger scores. This applies to any new accounts you open after discharge.

Building new positive history takes time. Rebuilding after bankruptcy is gradual, and there’s no substitute for steady, responsible credit habits. Our guide on how long it takes to rebuild credit after bankruptcy covers what to expect.

What FICO Score Ranges Are Possible After Bankruptcy?

Right after a filing, your score may fall into the “poor” range on the FICO scale (300 to 579). As positive history builds, moving into the “fair” range (580 to 669) and later the “good” range (670 to 739) may become possible if you manage new accounts responsibly.

How long it takes to reach “very good” (740 to 799) or “exceptional” (800+) varies widely. It depends on the type of bankruptcy, your habits after filing and what else is on your report.
